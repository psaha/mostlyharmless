\section{The two interactive models}
\label{sec:models}

The derivations are complete without this section, and nothing here is certified: what
follows is a pair of front ends and the visual checks that go with them.  They are recorded
because a picture can be wrong in ways a residual cannot --- a mirrored image satisfies every
statement in sections~\ref{sec:visibility} to~\ref{sec:full-map}, and did so for long enough
to be embarrassing --- and because a check that lives in prose is one that drifts away from
the code it describes.

Both models are Matplotlib windows over the same scaffolding: two panels, a shared colour
bar, sliders, radio groups, and a redraw that fires once when a slider drag ends rather than
once per mouse motion.  Each carries a \emph{preset} group: named parameter sets, labelled by
what you should see rather than by their angles, with a \code{manual} entry meaning ``these
are the user's own numbers'', re-selected whenever a slider moves.  The presets are the
reproducible part of the checks below.

\subsection{The deformation model}
\label{sec:model-deformation}

\code{make gui} shows a rotationally deformed star in the far field: a unit-radius source
whose level surface is the equipotential of a tide and a spin, with brightness fixed at the
von Zeipel law $I \propto g$.  The two panels are the near and far hemispheres.  The sliders
are the two amplitudes, the inclination, the tide's body latitude and longitude, and a limb
coefficient; the position angle of the spin is pinned to zero, being a gauge for a single
observation.

\paragraph{Why two sliders are missing.}  Two of the model's angles are gauges.  For a single
observation the sky's orientation is a choice, so the spin's position angle can be pinned.
And with a smooth deformation and no starspots, the spin phase $\alpha$ is degenerate with
the tide's longitude $\varphi$, because only their difference is observable: a rotation of the
star cannot be told from a rotation of the perturbation.  Both stop being gauges as soon as
the surface carries structure of its own --- spots see $\alpha$, and a non-synchronous tide
makes it independent.  That is why the map GUI of section~\ref{sec:model-map} has both
sliders and this one has neither.

The limb slider scales the brightness by $1 - u_\text{limb}(1-\mu)$, with
$\mu = \sqrt{1-\rho^2}$ the cosine of the angle to the line of sight, so that the centre stays
bright and the rim darkens.  One law is used by every row: the numeric row multiplies its
sampled brightness, the two fitted rows absorb it by fitting those samples, and the
first-order row carries it in closed form, since $\mu = \sqrt{4\pi/3}\,Y_{10}$ is a dipole
about the observer's axis and costs one coefficient at $m = 0$.  The rows therefore differ in
the shape and the brightness \emph{pattern}, which is what they are for, and not in the limb.
Only visible points are rendered, so $\mu > 0$ throughout and the law is monotone across the
disc.

The closed form follows from \eqref{eq:first-order-pattern} multiplied by the limb factor, and
it is derived in section~\ref{sec:deformation-first-order} together with the reason the
coefficients need no Gaunt integral.  What matters here is that only the numeric row's
brightness is sampled: the first-order row applies that algebra to the pattern, so the two
differ in the pattern and not in the limb.

One consequence should be stated rather than glossed: because the fit is made in the
\emph{body} frame, a star darkened by the viewing geometry is fitted as though the darkening
were painted onto the surface facing the observer.  The fitted coefficients are then
meaningful only at the orientation they were fitted for.  That is a limitation of the
surrogate's frame, not of the renderer: a basis whose coefficients did not absorb the viewing
angle would be needed to remove it.

The row group is the part worth explaining, because the rows differ in kind:
\begin{description}
\item[\code{numeric}] the exact level surface, solved by bisection along each sampled
  direction.  The reference.
\item[\code{first-order}] an ellipsoid, with the brightness linearised as in
  \eqref{eq:first-order-pattern}.  Against \code{numeric} this measures the whole analytic
  stack at $O(\epsilon)$.
\item[\code{fitted, l = 0,2,4}] coefficients chosen by least squares in the image plane,
  rather than taken from the brightness pattern.
\item[\code{fitted, l = 0,2,4,6,8}] the same at two more degrees.  Comparing the two fitted
  rows isolates the \emph{truncation} from the shape model's error, which the other pairs
  conflate.  At the ceiling the lower of the two still shows a hard edge where the brightness
  falls to zero, and the higher one genuinely does not: that is what a truncation floor looks
  like, and it is the reason the row is a diagnostic rather than another approximation.
\end{description}
The fitted rows print their residual in the panel titles.  It is not a defect of the fit but
the price of the degree: at fixed $\Lambda$ the residual is the truncation floor, and
section~\ref{sec:full-map} states why a discontinuous map converges slowly.

\paragraph{The presets, and what they test.}  \code{reference} is a moderate configuration,
$\epsilon_\text{tide} = 0.03$ and $\epsilon_\text{spin} = 0.01$, keeping
$\epsilon_\text{tide} + \tfrac32\epsilon_\text{spin} \approx 0.045$ well below the $0.148$
ceiling, with the spin axis inclined by $60°$ so that both the tide's bulge and the spin's
flattening are visible.  \code{ceiling} puts the same amplitudes in the equatorial plane,
which is the stress test for the analytic rows: the affine error reaches $0.11$ there.

\code{tilt check} is the geometry test.  The deformations are at the ceiling and the tide axis
is off both axes of the sky, at latitude $-45°$ and longitude $45°$, so the bulge lies on a
\emph{diagonal}.  That matters: an axis-aligned bulge maps to itself under a mirror, so a
sign error in the frame or in the display would pass unnoticed, while a diagonal one would
come out on the wrong diagonal.  Seen on the page, the stretching should run from lower right
to upper left --- mostly North with a little East, with the body-latitude bands
perpendicular to it --- and the picture is drawn with North up and East to the left, as
section~\ref{sec:conventions} fixes.

\subsection{The map model}
\label{sec:model-map}

\code{make mapgui-levels} shows the Earth as spherical-harmonic coefficients: a brightness map
carried in the body frame and rotated into the observer frame, in the manner of
section~\ref{sec:rotation-law}.  There is no deformation and no solve --- the model is a
\code{Coeffs} object --- so a redraw costs a coefficient sum, and the panels are the near and
far hemispheres in units of the mean.  This is the model to read first: one brightness, a
fixed map, and three angles.

\paragraph{Two levels, and why the ratio is the knob.}  Land and sea are carried at two fixed
brightnesses rather than as the colour image they were extracted from, because a two-level map
can be stated and checked in one sentence.  The levels are not chosen: the \emph{ratio} $r$
between land and sea is, and the condition that the sphere mean be one then fixes both,
\begin{equation}
  s = \frac{1}{1 + f(r-1)}, \qquad \text{land} = rs ,
  \label{eq:levels}
\end{equation}
with $f$ the land fraction measured from the image.  A ratio of $1.5$ gives $0.877$ and
$1.315$ at $f = 0.281$: a fifty per cent jump, which the eye resolves easily without making
the sea dark.

The ratio rather than the levels, because the step is what rings: the overshoot at a
truncation is a fraction of the step, so a $3{:}1$ contrast would ripple where $1.5$ does not.
The coefficients carry a Lanczos taper already, applied when they were written.  The
extraction finds $f = 0.281$ against the accepted $0.292$ of the Earth's surface, from a mask
that tests one colour against another --- close enough for a picture, and the levels adapt to
whatever it finds.

\paragraph{The sliders.}  The spin phase $\alpha$, the inclination $\beta$ and the sky's
position angle $\gamma$, in degrees, plus a degree radio and a resolution radio.  The map is
fixed, so these three are the whole of the interaction: the globe at rest, and then turned.
They open at $\alpha = 155°$ and $\beta = 75°$, which puts Africa towards the observer and
mostly Pacific on the far side.

\paragraph{The visibility view} draws the real and imaginary parts of $\Delta V$ against the
uniform disk.  Because the map is real, $V(-u,-v) = \overline{V(u,v)}$, so the real part is
even under $(u,v) \to (-u,-v)$ and the imaginary part odd \eqref{eq:hermitian} --- a check the
picture makes without a residual.  The values are small: the land--sea split is roughly
balanced between the hemispheres, so the low degrees carry little, while the baselines at
$q \sim 1$--$2$ are sensitive to exactly those degrees whose visibility the disk has already
taken to zero.

\paragraph{The retired starspot.}  An earlier model drew a polar cap on a uniform disk, with
sliders for the cap's half-angle, depth, colatitude and longitude, and four presets labelled
by where the spot should appear.  It went with the RGB version, kept at tag \code{rgb-mapgui};
what remains of it is \code{maps.cap\_coefficients}, which the certificates use as the only
closed-form coefficient set in the package.

\paragraph{Reading the page.}  Both models draw the sky with North up and East to the left, so
the screen carries the manual's $x$ and $y$ as its axes, and the third axis is the line of
sight.  Stated once, because every orientation question reduces to these:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Near side: $x$ up, $y$ to the left, $z$ out of the screen towards the observer.  That
    frame is right-handed as drawn, $x\times y = z$, which is the
    $(\text{North},\text{East},\text{observer})$ of section~\ref{sec:conventions}.
  \item Far side: the same page, with $z$ into the screen --- the visible surface's outward
    normal points away from you.  But the panel is the \emph{sky's mirror}, since a view from
    behind is what it shows, so its labels mean the mirrored directions and the sense of a
    rotation on that page is reversed.  Point 6 is the same statement.
  \item Increasing $\alpha$ turns the near-side picture \emph{anticlockwise}: a feature at the
    top moves towards the left.
  \item Increasing $\beta$ sends the middle of the near side \emph{upwards}, because it tilts
    the body's $+z$ towards North.
  \item $\gamma$ acts like $\alpha$ --- the same anticlockwise sense --- but about the line of
    sight and after $\beta$, since $R = R_z(\gamma)R_y(\beta)R_z(\alpha)$.
  \item On the far side every rotation appears \emph{reversed}, as a consequence of the
    mirroring in point 2.
\end{enumerate}
Points 3 and 5 are worth keeping together: $\alpha$ rotates the body and $\gamma$ rotates the
sky, and both turn the near-side picture the same way.  The half-turn between them is worth
knowing too: $\alpha$ and $\alpha + 180°$ show the same planet with the two faces exchanged,
since the rotation is about the spin axis and the pole does not move.

\subsection{What the checks are worth}
\label{sec:models-worth}

The two models are deliberately outside the certificate scheme.  A certificate compares two
routes to the same number; the checks here compare a picture with a stated expectation, and
their evidence is that a wrong answer is visible rather than that a residual is small.  That
is weaker, and it is why the presets exist: they move the expectation out of prose and into
the program, so that the geometry can be re-checked without anyone having to remember which
slider to move, or in which direction the answer should come out.
