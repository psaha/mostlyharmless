\section{Spherical harmonics}
\label{sec:harmonics}

The basis is real and is labelled by non-negative orders only, as set out in
section~\ref{sec:conventions}.  Complex harmonics appear in the derivations, also
only for $m \ge 0$.  Negative orders are never formed, so no magnitude is ever
needed and there is no signed-order convention to get wrong.  This section records
the facts the visibility derivation rests on, and says for each which part is
argument and which is algebra delegated to a certificate.

\subsection{Orthonormality}
\label{sec:orthonormality}

With negative orders defined by \eqref{eq:ylm-extension}, the complex harmonics
are orthonormal over the full range,
\begin{equation}
  \int_{S^2} Y_l^m \, \overline{Y_{l'}^{m'}} \, d\Omega
  = \delta_{ll'} \, \delta_{mm'} , \qquad -l \le m, m' \le l ,
  \label{eq:ylm-orthonormal}
\end{equation}
and the real basis \eqref{eq:real-basis} is orthonormal and complete:
\begin{equation}
  \int_{S^2} R_l^{m,\sigma} \, R_{l'}^{m',\sigma'} \, d\Omega
  = \delta_{ll'} \, \delta_{mm'} \, \delta_{\sigma\sigma'} ,
  \label{eq:real-orthonormal}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ runs over the branches $c$ and $s$, the $m = 0$ function counting
once on its own.

The proof of \eqref{eq:ylm-orthonormal} is two factors multiplied.  Writing
$d\Omega = \sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi$, the azimuthal factor is the elementary
Fourier statement
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^{2\pi} e^{i(m-m')\varphi} \, d\varphi = 2\pi \, \delta_{mm'} ,
  \label{eq:fourier-orthogonality}
\end{equation}
and the polar factor is
\begin{equation}
  \int_{-1}^{1} P_l^m(x) \, P_{l'}^m(x) \, dx
  = \delta_{ll'} \, \frac{2}{2l+1} \frac{(l+m)!}{(l-m)!} , \qquad m \ge 0 .
  \label{eq:legendre-orthogonality}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:legendre-orthogonality} is a polynomial identity.  Substituting
the Rodrigues form $P_l^m(x) = (1-x^2)^{m/2} Q_l^m(x)$ with
$Q_l^m = (-1)^m d^m P_l / dx^m$ turns it into an integral of a polynomial, which
certificate \vtag{1} evaluates exactly for every $0 \le m \le l \le 5$.
\emph{Exact}, range-limited to $l \le 5$.  The negative orders need no separate
treatment: by \eqref{eq:ylm-extension} they are conjugates of the positive ones up
to a sign, and conjugation commutes with the integral.

Multiplying \eqref{eq:fourier-orthogonality} by
\eqref{eq:legendre-orthogonality} gives \eqref{eq:ylm-orthonormal}: the $2\pi$ and
the normalisation together account for the surviving $2/(2l+1)$.  That
multiplication is the argument; the two identities are the certified part.  The
real basis then inherits \eqref{eq:real-orthonormal} directly, since
$R_l^{m,c}$ and $R_l^{m,s}$ are the real and imaginary parts of $Y_l^m$ scaled by
$\sqrt{2}$.

The implementation is checked end to end on a Gauss--Legendre grid in
$\cos\theta$ and the uniform Fourier rule in $\varphi$.  Both are exact for this
integrand, so the residual is floating point alone.  \emph{Numerical}, $l \le 4$.

The complex harmonics are also compared against SymPy's own \code{Ynm} at $30$
digits.  That is a convention check rather than an identity check, and it is done
numerically because the two expressions are algebraically equal but not
identically written --- a symbolic zero there would test the simplifier more than
the claim.

\subsection{The addition theorem}
\label{sec:addition}

In the real basis the addition theorem reads
\begin{equation}
  R_l^0(\hat n_1) R_l^0(\hat n_2)
  + \sum_{m=1}^{l} \Big[ R_l^{m,c}(\hat n_1) R_l^{m,c}(\hat n_2)
  + R_l^{m,s}(\hat n_1) R_l^{m,s}(\hat n_2) \Big]
  = \frac{2l+1}{4\pi} \, P_l(\cos\gamma) ,
  \label{eq:addition}
\end{equation}
with $\cos\gamma = \hat n_1 \cdot \hat n_2$.  Every index is non-negative.  The
form one usually meets in the literature,
$\sum_{m=-l}^{l} Y_l^m \overline{Y_l^m} = \frac{2l+1}{4\pi} P_l(\cos\gamma)$,
agrees with \eqref{eq:addition}: the negative orders there are exactly the
conjugates of the non-negative ones, so each $m > 0$ contributes its real and
imaginary parts separately, which is what the cosine and sine branches are.

This is classical and is cited rather than proved here.  Certificate \vtag{3}
checks our implementation of it, at $40$ digits and in floating point.
\emph{Numerical}, $l \le 5$ at $40$ digits.

An exact symbolic certificate is deliberately not attempted.  A symbolic route has
to reconcile $\sqrt{1-\cos^2\theta}$ with $\sin\theta$, at which point the
statement being checked is about branch assumptions rather than about the theorem.
Saying so is more useful than a certificate that passes for the wrong reason.

\subsection{Other conventions}
\label{sec:other-conventions}

Two translations are worth recording, for comparing with published work.

Most of the literature indexes real harmonics by a \emph{signed} order, with
$\cos(m\varphi)$ for $m > 0$ and $\sin(m\varphi)$ for $m < 0$.  That is a
relabelling of \eqref{eq:real-basis}: our $(l, m, c)$ and $(l, m, s)$ are their
$(l, m)$ and $(l, -m)$.  Interferometric surface-mapping codes, including the one
the analytic visibility work we build on uses, follow that convention.  Nothing in
this work needs it, but the translation is what makes published coefficients
directly comparable.

Second, complex coefficients for a real map are sometimes defined without the
$\sqrt{2}$ of \eqref{eq:half-complex}, in which case
\eqref{eq:map-from-half-complex} carries a different numerical factor.  The
relation above is the one used here, and it is the one pyshtools documents for the
coefficients of a real function.
