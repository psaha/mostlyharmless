\section{The deformed source: spin and tides}
\label{sec:triaxial}

Sections~\ref{sec:conventions}--\ref{sec:full-map} answer one question: given a surface
brightness map on the unit sphere, what is its complex visibility.  This section supplies
the map.  A star deformed by its own rotation and by a companion has a shape and a surface
brightness that are both consequences of a single potential, and the two are not
independent: on a level surface the normal lies along the gradient, so the surface gravity
follows from the shape.  The potential is written in dimensional form once, in
section~\ref{sec:deformation-potential}, and then rescaled: everything after that is
dimensionless, in units where $GM = 1$ and lengths are measured in the reference radius.

\subsection{The quadrupole potential}
\label{sec:deformation-potential}

The star is a monopole plus what its rotation and its companion do to it,
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\vecr) \;=\; -\frac{GM}{r}
    \;-\; \frac{GM'}{d^{3}}\,r^{2} P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvectide)
    \;+\; \frac{\Omega^{2}}{3}\,r^{2} P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvecspin)
    \;-\; \frac{\Omega^{2}}{3}\,r^{2}
  \label{eq:deformation-potential-dimensional}
\end{equation}
and each perturbation is worth deriving rather than quoting.

\paragraph{The rotation.}  In the frame that turns with the star at angular speed $\Omega$, a fluid
element at distance $r_\perp$ from the spin axis feels the centrifugal potential
\begin{equation}
  -\tfrac12\,\Omega^2 r_\perp^2
  \;=\; -\tfrac12\,\Omega^2 r^2\sin^2\theta
  \;=\; \tfrac13\,\Omega^2 r^2 P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvecspin)
  \;-\; \tfrac13\,\Omega^2 r^2
  \label{eq:centrifugal}
\end{equation}
the last step using $\sin^2\theta = \tfrac23\bigl(1 - P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvecspin)\bigr)$:
a quadrupole about the spin axis and a monopole of the same size.

\paragraph{The companion.}  A mass $M'$ at distance $d$ along $\uvectide$
contributes $-GM'/|\vecr-\mathbf d|$.  Expanded in $r/d$, its $\ell = 1$ term is a uniform
acceleration, removed by working in the star's free-fall frame, and its $\ell = 2$ term is the
quadrupole of \eqref{eq:deformation-potential-dimensional}.  Its $\ell = 0$ term is the constant
$GM'/d$; a gravitational field is harmonic, so no other constant appears, and a constant in
$\Phi$ shifts the level value rather than the shape, so it is dropped.

\paragraph{The response of the star.}  Everything above is the \emph{external} potential.  A real
body adds a distortion of its own, of the same shape, and two Love numbers say how much: $h_2$
is the radial displacement as a fraction of the forced one, $k_2$ the induced potential as a
fraction of the forcing.  A fluid body's surface must be an equipotential of the \emph{total}
potential, which forces
\begin{equation}
  h_2 \;=\; 1 + k_2
  \label{eq:love-relation}
\end{equation}
A star therefore has one Love number rather than two, running from $k_2 = 0$ for a centrally
condensed body to $k_2 = 3/2$ for a homogeneous one.  A solid body is where the two part --- the
Earth has $h_2 = 0.61$ against $1 + k_2 = 1.30$ --- and nothing here concerns it.

Since the displacement carries $h_2$ and nothing else in the shape does, we fold it into the
amplitudes and quote $\epsilon$ as the \emph{deformation} amplitude, not the potential's.  What
cannot be folded in is the $l = 0$ part, which carries $k_0 = 0$ --- a spherical redistribution
does not change the external field --- so it is not scaled with the rest.  That does not touch the
shape at all: it appears only in the surface gravity, where
section~\ref{sec:deformation-gravity} carries it explicitly and then sets $k_2 = 0$.

\paragraph{Rescaling.}  Every term of \eqref{eq:deformation-potential-dimensional} has the
dimensions of $GM/r$, so measuring length in units of a reference radius $a$ and the potential in
units of $GM/a$ leaves a dimensionless problem.  Written with
\begin{equation}
  \espin \;=\; (1 + k_2)\,\frac{\Omega^{2}a^{3}}{3GM} , \qquad
  \etide \;=\; (1 + k_2)\,\frac{M'}{M}\Bigl(\frac{a}{d}\Bigr)^{3}
  \label{eq:deformation-amplitudes}
\end{equation}
and dividing $\Phi$ by $GM/a$, the potential is
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\vecr) \;=\; -\frac{1}{r}
    \;-\; \etide\, r^2 P_2(\uvecr\cdot \uvectide)
    \;+\; \espin\, r^2 P_2(\uvecr\cdot \uvecspin)
    \;-\; \espin\, r^2
  \label{eq:deformation-potential}
\end{equation}
\emph{This} is the form used from here on: $GM$ is no longer written, the monopole is $-1/r$, and
lengths are in units of the unperturbed radius, which is therefore $1$.  The level value is a
different number: it settles at $a = 1 + O(\epsilon)$, and
section~\ref{sec:deformation-gravity} carries it explicitly.  Fixing the unperturbed radius fixes
no gauge.

Both quadrupoles are pure $l = 2$, and the amplitudes are positive for a tidal bulge along
$\uvectide$ and a centrifugal flattening about $\uvecspin$.
The last term of \eqref{eq:deformation-potential} is the spin's monopole; unlike the tide's
constant it is $r^2$ rather than constant, so it cannot be dropped: it renormalises the
effective monopole, and omitting it would leave the mean radius wrong by $\espin$.

The amplitudes are dimensionless: $\epsilon = 0.2$ means a twenty per cent deformation of the
\emph{potential} against the monopole at unit radius, whatever axis ratio that produces.  The
surface is the level set $\Phi = -1$ up to the gauge of section~\ref{sec:deformation-closure}.
The volume-equivalent sphere is a separate quantity, since the deformation changes the volume,
and the two should not be confused when quoting an axis ratio.

\subsection{The level surface, and the gauge}
\label{sec:deformation-closure}

Along a direction $\uvecr$ the level condition is one equation in one unknown,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{r} + E(\uvecr)\, r^2 \;=\; \frac{1}{a}
  \label{eq:level-condition}
\end{equation}
where the depth of the perturbation there is
\begin{equation}
  E(\uvecr) \;=\; \etide P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvectide)
           - \espin\bigl[P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvecspin) - 1\bigr]
  \label{eq:level-depth}
\end{equation}
The sign of the spin term is negative because its coefficient in
\eqref{eq:deformation-potential} is positive while it \emph{lowers} the potential.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[$\checkmark$] \code{check_level_surface} evaluates $\Phi$ on the returned surface and
    compares $\nabla\Phi$ there with a central difference, so a wrong sign in
    \eqref{eq:deformation-potential} fails at once.  It holds at any amplitude.
    \\[1pt] \vtag{20}\quad \emph{Numerical, working amplitudes}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The gauge.}  The level value $a$ is ours to choose, and it is the star's size.  Take
it so that the angular mean of $r$ is exactly $1$.  Expanding \eqref{eq:level-condition} to first
order then gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\delta r}{a} \;=\; \bigl(E(\uvecr) - \langle E\rangle\bigr)\,a^{3}
  \label{eq:gauge-displacement}
\end{equation}
with $\langle\cdot\rangle$ the angular average, so the displacement is a \emph{pure quadrupole}:
the $l = 0$ part of $E$ has gone into the level value and no longer distorts the shape.  Since
$\langle E\rangle = \espin$, that condition fixes the first-order part of $a$; the
freedom at order $\epsilon k_2$ is spent on making the surface gravity free of $k_2$
(section~\ref{sec:deformation-gravity}),
\begin{equation}
  a \;=\; 1 - \espin\,(1 + k_2) + O(\epsilon^{2})
  \label{eq:gauge-radius}
\end{equation}
which declares the mean radius to be $1 - \espin{}k_2$ rather than $1$ --- inside the
remainder the model drops.  With $a = 1$ the displacement is $\delta r = E - \langle E\rangle$.

\subsection{The surface gravity}
\label{sec:deformation-gravity}

The gradient is worth writing out explicitly.  The $\Phi$ of
\eqref{eq:deformation-potential} simplifies once $r^2(\uvecr\cdot\uvece)^2 =
(\vecr\cdot\uvece)^2$ is used to move the $r$-dependence out of the Legendre
polynomials:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\vecr) \;=\; -\frac{1}{r}
    \;+\; \tfrac12(\etide - 3\espin)\,r^2
    \;-\; \tfrac32\etide\,(\vecr\cdot\uvectide)^2
    \;+\; \tfrac32\espin\,(\vecr\cdot\uvecspin)^2
  \label{eq:deformation-potential-quadratic}
\end{equation}
which differentiates term by term to
\begin{equation}
  \nabla\Phi \;=\; \frac{\vecr}{r^3}
    \;+\; (\etide - 3\espin)\,\vecr
    \;-\; 3\etide\,(\vecr\cdot\uvectide)\,\uvectide
    \;+\; 3\espin\,(\vecr\cdot\uvecspin)\,\uvecspin
  \label{eq:deformation-gradient}
\end{equation}
Evaluating \eqref{eq:deformation-gradient} at $\vecr = r(\uvecr)\,\uvecr$, with $r(\uvecr)$ from
\eqref{eq:level-condition}, and taking its norm gives $g(\uvecr)$.  The direction of
$\nabla\Phi$ is of course the outward normal used for the visibility sign in
section~\ref{sec:deformation-exact}, so the same expression serves both purposes.

Two checks.  With both amplitudes zero the first term survives and $|\nabla\Phi| = 1/r^2$,
which is $1$ at $r = 1$ as required.  To first order, linearise $g$ itself rather than working
by routes:
\begin{equation}
  g \;=\; \bigl|\nabla\Phi\bigr| \;=\; \frac{1}{r^{2}} - 2\,E\,r
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \frac{\delta g}{g_0} \;=\; -2\,\frac{\delta r}{a} \;-\; 2\,E\,a^{3}
  \label{eq:deformation-gravity-shift}
\end{equation}
the first term being the monopole evaluated off-centre and the second the perturbation's own
radial derivative.  The tangential part of $\nabla(E r^{2})$ is perpendicular to
$\uvecr$ and enters only at $O(\epsilon^{2})$, which is why it is absent.  For a
perturbation growing as $r^{\ell}$ the second term is $\ell\,Ea$; a quadrupole has $\ell = 2$,
so the two contributions carry equal weight, and with $\delta r$ substituted from
\eqref{eq:gauge-displacement} they carry the same sign as well.

With $a = 1$ the result is
\begin{equation}
  g \;=\; 1 - 2\espin
    - 4\Bigl[\etide\,P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvectide)
    - \espin\,P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvecspin)\Bigr]
    + O(\epsilon^{2})
  \label{eq:deformation-gravity-first-order}
\end{equation}
The quadrupole is $-4q$, as the $\ell + 2$ rule requires, and neither term carries $k_2$: the
amplitudes absorbed it in the quadrupole, and the gauge \eqref{eq:gauge-radius} absorbed it in the
constant.  The constant is the $l = 0$ part of the perturbation surviving where the shape cannot
reach it --- the centrifugal potential lowers the potential everywhere, so it lowers the mean
effective gravity with it.

\paragraph{What absorbing $k_2$ costs.}  The gauge of \eqref{eq:gauge-radius} is only a
convention: it declares the mean radius to be $1 - \espin{}k_2$ rather than $1$,
a difference of order $\epsilon^{2}$ because a star has $k_2 \sim 10^{-2}$.  That is inside the
remainder the model already drops.  For a body that was not centrally condensed --- a homogeneous
one has $k_2 = 3/2$ --- the difference would be of order $\epsilon$ and could not be absorbed this
way; a star is safe because it is close to condensed, not because the argument is general.

The sign is the physically expected one: $g$ is larger at the poles than at the equator by
$6\espin$, twice from the displacement and four times from the quadrupole's own
gradient.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[$\checkmark$] \code{check_deformation_first_order} compares \eqref{eq:deformation-gravity-shift}
    and the displacement with $|\nabla\Phi|$ from \eqref{eq:deformation-gradient}, at both signs of
    the perturbation.
    \\[1pt] \vtag{19}\quad \emph{Algebraic}
\end{itemize}
\subsection{The limits on the amplitudes}
\label{sec:deformation-limits}

Two things bound the model: the level surface must exist, and the star must rotate below
break-up.  The first binds first, and by a wide margin once the two axes are perpendicular.

\paragraph{Closure.}  The left side of \eqref{eq:level-condition} is not monotone in $r$: the monopole falls and
the quadrupole grows.  Its minimum is at $r^3 = 1/(2E)$ and equals
$1.89\,E^{1/3}$, so a solution exists only while
\begin{equation}
  E(\uvecr) \;<\; 0.148 \qquad \text{for every } \uvecr ,
  \qquad 0.148 = (1/1.89)^3
  \label{eq:closure}
\end{equation}
Past that the surface opens along the deepest direction, as a Roche lobe does.  This is a
\emph{physical} limit of the model, not a numerical one, and the amplitude that triggers it
is the useful result: in the perpendicular case, where the depth along the tide axis is
\begin{equation}
  E \;=\; \etide + 1.5\,\espin
  \label{eq:equatorial-depth}
\end{equation}
the bound reads $0.148$ for the tide alone, $0.099$ for the spin alone (whose coefficient
is $1.5\espin$), and $0.059$ for two equal amplitudes.  A twenty per cent
deformation of the potential, which was the first target, is therefore not attainable in
this model at all.

When the two axes are not perpendicular but separated by an angle $\theta$, the deepest
direction is found from the quadratic form \eqref{eq:level-depth} above, and the depth
becomes
\begin{equation}
  E_\text{max} \;=\; \tfrac34\Bigl[(\epsilon_t-\epsilon_s)
    + \sqrt{(\epsilon_t-\epsilon_s)^2 + 4\epsilon_t\epsilon_s\cos^2\theta}\Bigr]
    + \tfrac{3\epsilon_s-\epsilon_t}{2}
  \label{eq:depth-latitude}
\end{equation}
which reduces to \eqref{eq:equatorial-depth} at $\theta = \pi/2$ and to
$\etide$ alone at $\theta = 0$.  A tide aligned with the spin costs nothing in
the centrifugal term, which has no gradient along the spin axis, so tilting the tide
\emph{loosens} the bound.  That is the opposite of the intuition that a more oblique system
is more constrained, and it is what makes the latitude a useful parameter rather than a
nuisance.

\paragraph{Break-up.}  The spin amplitude and the rotation rate are one quantity,
$\espin = \Omega^2/3$, so a star at break-up --- where centrifugal support
balances gravity at the equator --- would have $\espin = \tfrac13$.  The figures of \eqref{eq:closure} are below that, and comfortably so for the spin
alone: the truncated potential opens before the star could rotate apart.  That is a statement
about the quadrupole truncation, not about stars --- the true Roche surface closes at every
rotation rate, because the higher multipoles the expansion drops are what keep it closed.

\subsection{The numerical row}
\label{sec:deformation-exact}

The brightness follows from the gravity, and for the undeformed star $g = 1$, so it falls
out dimensionless:
\begin{equation}
  I \;\propto\; g^{p} , \qquad g = |\nabla\Phi|
  \label{eq:brightness-law}
\end{equation}

The bolometric von Zeipel law is $p = 1$.  Physically motivated laws have $p \le 1$: a
convective envelope is nearer to $p \approx 0.3$, because the flux there is carried by
convection and so responds to gravity more weakly.  Thus $p = 1$ is an upper bound rather
than a convenient default, and it is the one the code uses.

The brightness is evaluated on the surface at each sample, so what the row needs of it is its
value there and nothing else; $I(x,y)$ may in general come from a stellar-atmosphere model with no
simple form.  An instance, and the one the figures and every row below use, adds a linear limb
factor to the gravity,
\begin{equation}
  I(x,y) \;=\; \bigl(1 - u_\text{limb}\,(1 - z/r)\bigr)\, g(x,y) ,
  \label{eq:brightness-example}
\end{equation}
with $z/r$ the viewing cosine of the surface point that projects to $(x,y)$ and $g$ its surface
gravity.  \eqref{eq:brightness-law} is its $u_\text{limb} = 0$ case.

With the surface and the brightness fixed, the visibility is the integral of
section~\ref{sec:mode-visibility} over the sky plane.  The level surface carries it to an integral
over directions,
\begin{equation}
  V(u,v) \;=\; \int_{\uvecn\cdot\uvecz > 0}
    I(\uvecr)\,(\uvecn\cdot\uvecz)\,
    e^{-2\pi i (u x + v y)}\; dS ,
  \label{eq:deformation-visibility-integral}
\end{equation}
with $dS$ the element of the level surface and $d^2x = (\uvecn\cdot\uvecz)\,dS$
its projection onto the sky plane.  The solid angle enters only through
$dS = r^2\,d\Omega/(\uvecr\cdot\uvecn)$, the tilted patch being larger than its
spherical shadow by $1/(\uvecr\cdot\uvecn)$.

The quadrature replaces $dS$ by the surface element each sample owns, which for evenly spread
directions is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta S_i \;=\; \frac{r_i^2\,\Delta\Omega_i}{\uvecr_i\cdot\uvecn_i} ,
  \qquad \Delta\Omega_i = \frac{4\pi}{N} ,
  \label{eq:deformation-surface-element}
\end{equation}
so that the sum is
\begin{equation}
  V(u,v) \;=\; \sum_i I(\uvecr_i)\,(\uvecn_i\cdot\uvecz)\,
    \Delta S_i\; e^{-2\pi i (u x_i + v y_i)}
  \label{eq:deformation-visibility}
\end{equation}
which is the form the code evaluates.

Two properties of a level surface make this cheap.  The outward normal is the gradient direction,
$\uvecn = \nabla\Phi/|\nabla\Phi|$, so a sample is visible exactly when
$\uvecn\cdot\uvecz > 0$: there is no z-buffer and no nested root find.  And for
modest deformation the body is convex, so the map from direction to sky position is one-to-one.

Equation~\eqref{eq:deformation-visibility} is a quadrature, not a periodic transform, so its
error is ordinary discretisation error that falls with the sampling.  It carries none of the
Gibbs ringing at the limb that an FFT would impose, which is why the direct sum, and not an
FFT, is the reference for every certificate in this part.  The level radii come from a
bisection that brackets the \emph{inner} crossing of \eqref{eq:level-condition}: the left
side is not monotone, and the outer branch is the far side of the lobe.

The strongest check in this part is not a tagged certificate but the null case: a zero
deformation must render as the uniform disk, with flux $\pi$ and the profile
$2J_1(x)/x$ of section~\ref{sec:uniform-disk}.  The oblate case then reproduces both the
equatorial bulge and the reduced equatorial gravity from the one field, which is the
statement that shape and gravity are not independent.

\subsection{The principal axes and the ellipsoid}
\label{sec:deformation-axes}

Expanding the Legendre polynomials in \eqref{eq:level-depth} and simplifying puts the depth
in monomial form,
\begin{equation}
  E(\uvecr) \;=\; \tfrac32\Bigl[
    \etide\,(\uvecr\cdot\uvectide)^2
    - \espin\,(\uvecr\cdot\uvecspin)^2\Bigr]
    \;+\; \tfrac12\bigl(3\espin - \etide\bigr)
  \label{eq:depth-form}
\end{equation}
with the common factor $\tfrac32$ on the squared projections and the constant left outside.  Since
$(\uvecr\cdot\uvece)^2 = \uvecr^{T}
(\uvece\otimes\uvece)\uvecr$, the bracket is the quadratic form of
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{M} \;=\; \etide\,
    \uvectide\otimes\uvectide
    \;-\; \espin\,
    \uvecspin\otimes\uvecspin
  \label{eq:depth-tensor}
\end{equation}
a symmetric matrix with no numerical factor in it, so that
\begin{equation}
  E(\uvecr) \;=\; \tfrac32\;\uvecr^{T}\cdot\mathbf{M}\cdot\uvecr
    \;+\; \tfrac12\bigl(3\espin - \etide\bigr)
  \label{eq:depth-quadratic}
\end{equation}
The constant shifts every depth by the same amount and moves no eigenvector.  The principal
directions are therefore the eigenvectors of $\mathbf{M}$ itself, and the depths along them are
$\tfrac32$ times its eigenvalues plus that constant.

The principal axes of the level surface are those eigenvectors.  With the gauge of
\eqref{eq:gauge-displacement} the displacement is a pure quadrupole, so the surface is an
\emph{ellipsoid} to first order --- not an approximation imposed on it but what the level
condition says --- with its axes along those eigenvectors and its scale the mean radius.
The displacement being a pure quadrupole, it is traceless: the three semi-axes depart from the
mean radius by amounts whose sum is zero, so their product is $1 + O(\epsilon^{2})$ and the
volume changes only at second order.  The shape is fixed by two numbers, the third axis being
set by the mean radius.

The semi-axes are the exact level radii along those directions, one root each.  They are \emph{not}
taken from $\mathbf{M}$: the eigenvalues give the directions and nothing else.  Linearising the
radii instead would be closed form, but it is exact only to first order, and at the working
amplitudes it departs from the level surface by an amount the rows detect --- the fitted row's
flux identity and the first-order row's order test both fail when it is used, at the per-cent
level for $\epsilon \approx 0.03$.  The three roots are the cheaper error.

\subsection{The stretch, and what it can and cannot model}
\label{sec:deformation-stretch}

The stretch replaces the sky map \eqref{eq:deformation-visibility-integral} by a linear one.  We
want an affine map $\Lambda$ carrying the unit circle onto the ellipse that the ellipsoid projects
to on the sky, built from the ellipsoid's own axes rather than by the standard quadratic-form
route.

First the ellipsoid.  Its semi-axes are $r_k$ along the principal directions
$\uvece_k$ of section~\ref{sec:deformation-axes}, and the $r_k$ are found numerically from
the level surface, one root per direction: this is where the approximation enters, and the only
place it does.  The matrix carrying the unit sphere onto the ellipsoid is
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda_{\mathrm{3D}} \;=\; \sum_k r_k\,\uvece_k\otimes\uvece_k ,
  \label{eq:deformation-stretch-3d}
\end{equation}
symmetric positive definite, with those eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Second, the projection.  The observer sees the shadow on the sky plane, so the $\uvecz$
row is discarded and $\Lambda_{\mathrm{3D}}$ becomes the $2\times3$ matrix $P\Lambda_{\mathrm{3D}}$
carrying the $(\uvecx,\uvecy)$ rows.

Third, the stretch.  The shadow's matrix is the Gram of that restricted map, and $\Lambda$ is its
symmetric square root,
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda \;=\; \sqrt{(P\Lambda_{\mathrm{3D}})(P\Lambda_{\mathrm{3D}})^{T}} ,
  \label{eq:deformation-stretch}
\end{equation}
which carries the unit circle onto the shadow.  That square root is not the only map with the
property --- the circle is rotation-invariant, so $\Lambda R$ for any orthogonal $R$ does as well
--- but the choice has no content.  The silhouette and the flux are fixed by
$\Lambda\Lambda^{T}$ alone, and the model is a change of coordinates, so a different choice moves
the difference into the coefficients of whatever series is built on it.

Writing $V_\text{sph}$ for
the visibility of a sphere carrying the same brightness pattern, the surrogate is
\begin{equation}
  V_\text{affine}(u,v) \;=\; V_\text{sph}\!\left(\Lambda^{T}(u,v)\right)
  \label{eq:deformation-affine}
\end{equation}
There is \emph{no flux factor}.  The change of variables that stretches the image puts
$1/|\det\Lambda|$ on the brightness and $|\det\Lambda|$ on the measure, and the two cancel;
in the normalised quantity \eqref{eq:normalised-visibility} the determinant cancels
again.  So only the shape is modelled, while the brightness pattern --- gravity darkening
included --- is carried exactly.

$\Lambda$ is best read as a \emph{preconditioner} rather than as part of the answer.  It enters
twice --- in the frequency argument above and in the pull-back of the image --- and a different
but equally defensible $\Lambda$ changes the coefficients of any series built on it without
changing the exact answer; only the rate at which a truncated series converges depends on it.
Circularising the silhouette well is what makes a short series possible.

\paragraph{The same stretch in image space.}  The frequency change has a counterpart in the
image, and stating it makes the absent flux factor intuitive:
\begin{equation}
  V_\text{new}(u) = V(\Lambda^{T}u)
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  I_\text{new}(y) = \frac{1}{|\det\Lambda|}\,I\bigl(\Lambda^{-T}y\bigr)
  \label{eq:affine-image-transform}
\end{equation}
A feature at $x$ therefore appears at $y = \Lambda x$: the image is \emph{stretched} by
$\Lambda$, and its intensity is \emph{divided} by $|\det\Lambda|$, which exactly compensates
the area growth and leaves the total flux unchanged.  Since $\Lambda$ is symmetric positive
definite, $\Lambda^{T} = \Lambda$ and $\Lambda^{-T} = \Lambda^{-1}$: frequencies are
multiplied by $\Lambda$ while positions are divided by it.  For a general invertible $M$ the
two would be $Mu$ and $M^{-T}y$, which is why \eqref{eq:deformation-affine} is written with a
transpose; with this $\Lambda$ the transpose is cosmetic.

The model drops the Jacobian deliberately.  It moves the sample positions by $\Lambda$,
divides their brightness by $|\det\Lambda|$ and multiplies their weights by the same factor,
so the two cancel: what reaches the image is the stretched pattern with its brightness values
intact, and the image's total flux grows by $|\det\Lambda|$ instead of staying fixed.  That is
harmless because the discarded factor is a constant and $\Delta V$
\eqref{eq:normalised-visibility} is normalised at the origin --- and it is the honest
statement of the model's claim, which concerns shape and pattern rather than flux.

Two orderings have to be kept apart, and getting either wrong produces an error that looks
plausible.  The first is $\Lambda$'s own: it is expressed in the sky's
$(\uvecy,\uvecx)$ order, the order section~\ref{sec:conventions} gives the sky
axes and the order the image path uses, whereas the frequencies are $(u,v) =
(\text{North},\text{East})$.  The frequency is therefore reordered into the sky's order
before the stretch acts, and the result read back as $(\text{North},\text{East})$.  One
$2\times2$ matrix cannot serve both orders.  Using it in the wrong one transposes the
stretch, which is invisible for a nearly circular $\Lambda$ --- the pure-spin case --- and
wrong by order the anisotropy for anything else.

$V_\text{sph}$ is the mode sum of section~\ref{sec:mode-visibility}, evaluated on the
brightness pattern projected onto the harmonics.  Keeping $l \le 2$ is not a compromise: at
first order in the deformation the pattern is exactly $l = 0$ and $l = 2$, since the
$l = 4$ content first appears at second order from $P_{2,2}$.

No odd $l$ appears at any order, and the reason is symmetry rather than truncation.  Every
term of \eqref{eq:deformation-potential} is even under $n \to -n$ --- the monopole depends
on $r$ alone, and the others are squares of projections --- so $r(\uvecr)$ and $g(\uvecr)$ are even
functions of direction.  Since $Y_{lm}(-\uvecr) = (-1)^l Y_{lm}(\uvecr)$, an even function carries only
even $l$, and the argument is frame-independent because the point reflection is.  Spin and
the first-order tide therefore produce $l = 0$ and $l = 2$ and nothing else, in the body
frame and the observer frame alike; an $l = 1$ term would be a photocentre displacement,
which a deformation with this reflection symmetry cannot generate.  (A projection loop that
runs over $l = 1$ will still fill that slot, but with quadrature noise: it should shrink
with the sample count, and anything that does not is a real asymmetry and a bug.)

\paragraph{What a better shape would not fix.}  Expanding the level surface to the next order
\eqref{eq:level-condition} to second order in the deformation instead of stopping at the
ellipsoid, bringing $l = 4$ into the shape.  It would improve the outline at large amplitude,
where the ellipsoid visibly disagrees with the level surface.  It would \emph{not} repair the
stretch's error, because that error is first order and comes from the stretch, not from the
shape's second-order terms: a better ellipsoid does not make a linear map able to reproduce a
radial-nonlinear one.  The second-order term therefore stays documented rather than implemented.

\subsection{The first-order row}
\label{sec:deformation-first-order}

The fit of section~\ref{sec:deformation-fitted} is general: it will represent any pattern the
disk harmonics can carry, and it costs a solve per parameter set.  What follows is the special case the deformation actually produces at
leading order, where the pattern is a constant and two quadrupoles, so the coefficients are known
in closed form and nothing is fitted at all.

\paragraph{The coefficients in closed form.}  To first order the pattern is a constant plus
two quadrupoles --- \eqref{eq:deformation-gravity-first-order} with $p = 1$ ---
\begin{equation}
  I \;=\; (1 - 2\espin)
    \;-\; 4\etide\,P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvectide)
    \;+\; 4\espin\,P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvecspin)
  \label{eq:first-order-pattern}
\end{equation}
Each $P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvece)$ is a zonal $l = 2$ pattern about its own axis, so the corollary
\eqref{eq:zonal-l2} of section~\ref{sec:rotation-law} turns the bracket into harmonics
outright,
\begin{equation}
  I(\uvecr) \;=\; 1 - 2\espin
    - \frac{4}{5}\sum_{m=-2}^{2}\bigl(
      \etide\,Y_{2m}^*(\uvectide)
      - \espin\,Y_{2m}^*(\uvecspin)\bigr)
      Y_{2m}(\uvecr)
  \label{eq:first-order-harmonics}
\end{equation}
in the observer frame.  Nothing in that display is a coefficient yet: it is the pattern, with
the addition theorem applied and the terms left where they fell.  Collecting them, the
coefficients are
\begin{equation}
  a_{00} \;=\; 1 - 2\espin , \qquad
  a_{2m} \;=\; -\frac{4}{5}\Bigl[\etide\,
      Y_{2m}^*(\uvectide)
    \;-\; \espin\,Y_{2m}^*(\uvecspin)\Bigr]
  \label{eq:first-order-coefficients}
\end{equation}
every other coefficient being zero, with the two axes referred to the observer frame by the
same rule as a sampled direction, \eqref{eq:observer-angles}.  So this row of the model
needs \emph{no projection at all}: ten harmonic evaluations and one constant, with only the
level radii entering $\Lambda$ still numerical.  The first-order row of the model evaluates
\eqref{eq:first-order-coefficients} directly, so that row costs ten harmonic evaluations rather
than a projection.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[$\checkmark$] \code{check_first_order_coefficients} compares \eqref{eq:first-order-coefficients}
    against the quadrature that projects the same pattern, so a wrong sign or constant shows up at
    the size of the coefficients rather than at the $10^{-5}$ discretisation leaves.  It calls the
    function the model calls, so it covers the code and not a copy of it.
    \\[1pt] \vtag{21}\quad \emph{Numerical, at roundoff}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{One formula, and a certificate for the frame.}  The pattern is fixed by two axes and
two amplitudes, all frame-independent objects, so \eqref{eq:first-order-coefficients} holds in
any frame: the model carries the axes as the body has them and rotates the coefficients
afterwards, while the row evaluates the same formula with the axes already rotated.  The two
must agree.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[$\checkmark$] \code{check_first_order_frame} compares the two routes above.  They share the
    $l = 2$ corollary, so a wrong constant would pass and a wrong frame rule would not --- which is
    what makes it a test of the frame rule rather than of the formula.
    \\[1pt] \vtag{24}\quad \emph{Algebraic}
\end{itemize}

One is tempted to linearise the radii as well, replacing the bisection by $\delta r = E$ --- the
first term of \eqref{eq:radius-series} --- and making the row analytic all the way through.  The temptation should be resisted.  First it
is exact only to first order, and the next term is not small: expanding
\eqref{eq:level-condition} gives
\begin{equation}
  \delta r \;=\; E + 3E^2 + \cdots
  \label{eq:radius-series}
\end{equation}
so at the amplitude ceiling, where $E = \etide + 1.5\espin$ reaches
$0.144$ against the pinch at $0.148$, the linearised radius is $0.144$ where the cubic gives
$0.366$ --- a bulge barely two fifths of the true one.  The series is asymptotic, and the
ceiling is precisely where it diverges.  Second, the saving would be three bisections per
parameter set, some fifty scalar evaluations, against the $10^5$ that the quadrature it
replaces used to cost: nothing measurable.  A shortcut that is safe only in a corner and buys
nothing is a hazard rather than an option, so it is not offered at all.  The model uses the
exact radii and linearises only the brightness, which keeps the difference between the two
analytic rows a genuine ``$O(\epsilon^2)$'' comparison rather than a shape error in disguise.

Each frequency therefore costs a
handful of Bessel functions, which is the whole point: the projection is paid once per
parameter set and the per-frequency cost is negligible, so a search over orientations is
cheap even at a sparse set of baselines.

The second ordering is the projection's, and it is worth stating because it is the point at
which the surrogate has most often gone wrong.  The pattern must be projected in the
\emph{observer} frame, not the body frame.  Let $(\uvecx,\uvecy)$ be the sky axes
of section~\ref{sec:conventions} for the viewing direction, and take as polar axis
\begin{equation}
  \uvecz \;=\; \uvecx \times \uvecy
  \label{eq:observer-polar-axis}
\end{equation}
the direction \emph{toward} the observer.  That identity is not free: it holds only when the
sky's North is the projection of the body's North axis and the sky's East is
$\uvecz\times\text{North}$, the pairing section~\ref{sec:conventions} fixes.  Taking
East from a helper axis instead gives a North that is the projection of \emph{minus} North ---
the same handedness, but rotated by $\pi$ in the sky plane.  The consequence is a reflection of
the sphere, and since $Y_{lm}(\pi-\theta,\varphi) = (-1)^{l+m}Y_{lm}(\theta,\varphi)$ it leaves
every $|a_{lm}|$ unchanged while negating the $m = 1$ coefficients and leaving $m = 2$ alone:
the coefficients look plausible, every imaginary part has the wrong sign, and only a
diagnostic that compares two routes to the same number shows it.  Then
for each sampled direction $\vecr_i$,
\begin{equation}
  \theta_i = \arccos(\vecr_i\cdot\uvecr) , \qquad
  \varphi_i = \operatorname{atan2}(\vecr_i\cdot\uvecy,\, \vecr_i\cdot\uvecx)
  \label{eq:observer-angles}
\end{equation}
and $a_{lm} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i I_i Y_{lm}^*(\theta_i,\varphi_i)$ is a plain
quadrature, exactly as in section~\ref{sec:mode-visibility}.

Projecting in the body frame and rotating the coefficients afterwards does not do instead.
A rotation such as $R_y(i)$ fixes where $\hat z$ goes and leaves the azimuthal reference
somewhere else, so the frame is wrong by a rotation: the real and imaginary parts of $V$
then differ from the direct sum by a quarter turn.  In the axisymmetric case it fails more
quietly still --- the pattern stays axisymmetric about the line of sight and its visibility
is exactly real, so the imaginary part vanishes identically rather than being wrong.  Both
failures are what the undeformed null test and the direct-sum comparison of
section~\ref{sec:deformation-fitted} are for.

The approximation is nonetheless \emph{first order}, and that is worth stating precisely
because it is easy to assume otherwise.  Comparing \eqref{eq:deformation-affine} against
\eqref{eq:deformation-visibility} on the same samples gives a residual of $0.0016$ at the
working amplitudes and $0.0208$ with the amplitudes halved, a ratio of $0.48$; with the
deformation switched off entirely the residual is zero to printed precision.  The error
therefore scales as $\epsilon$, not $\epsilon^2$, and it is the linearisation of the map
\eqref{eq:deformation-visibility} --- a stretch cannot reproduce a map that is
radial-nonlinear --- rather than a defect in the projection.

It is not, however, simply proportional to $\epsilon$.  At the amplitude ceiling, where
$E = 0.144$, the same comparison gives $0.3975$: the coefficient grows from about $1.3E$ to
$2.8E$ between the two regimes.  That is what an asymptotic series with a large next term
should do --- the radius expansion \eqref{eq:radius-series} already carries $3E^2$, and $E$
is approaching the pinch at $0.148$ where the surface opens.  So the surrogate is a
few-per-cent instrument for $\epsilon \lesssim 0.03$ and a forty-per-cent one at the ceiling.

\subsection{Limb darkening}
\label{sec:deformation-brightness}

The numerical row has applied limb darkening sample by sample since
\eqref{eq:brightness-example}.  What that row does not need is any algebra of the factor; what the
first-order row does need is its projection onto the disk basis, and that is what this section
supplies.  The factor itself is the same everywhere: the sampled brightness, the linearised
pattern and the fitted coefficients all carry
\begin{equation}
  1 - u_\text{limb}\,(1-\mu)
  \label{eq:limb-factor}
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is a different thing in each row.  The numerical row samples the surface, so its
cosine is the surface's own,
\begin{equation}
  \mu \;=\; \frac{z}{r} \;=\; \sqrt{1 - \frac{\rho^{2}}{r^{2}}} ,
  \label{eq:limb-cosine}
\end{equation}
$r$ being the distance to that sample.  The model rows place the factor inside the disk basis and so need it at the disk coordinate, which is where $\Lambda$ enters:
\begin{equation}
  \mu \;=\; \sqrt{1 - \bigl|\Lambda^{-1}(x,y)\bigr|^{2}} ,
  \label{eq:model-cosine}
\end{equation}
the sphere's cosine at the unstretched position.  The two agree to first order in $\epsilon$,
since $r = 1 + O(\epsilon)$; the difference is counted in \eqref{eq:row-error}.  Of the model rows,
the closed-form one carries the limb exactly, in the coefficients of \eqref{eq:limb-coefficients},
while the fitted row absorbs it into the coefficients: its basis $\Yup_{lm}$ is limb-free.  With $u_\text{limb} = 0$ the factor recovers
\eqref{eq:brightness-law} and with $u_\text{limb} = 1$ it darkens the limb to nothing.

The pattern of \eqref{eq:brightness-example} is the first-order brightness of
section~\ref{sec:deformation-first-order}.  Multiplying it by the limb factor
\eqref{eq:limb-factor}, and writing that factor as $1 - u_\text{limb} + u_\text{limb}\mu$, gives
\begin{equation}
  I(\uvecr) \;=\; \bigl[(1 - u_\text{limb}) + u_\text{limb}\,\mu\bigr]\,
  \Bigl[\,1 - 2\espin
  - 4\etide\,P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvectide)
  + 4\espin\,P_2(\uvecr\cdot\uvecspin) \Bigr] ,
  \label{eq:limb-pattern}
\end{equation}
which is a product of harmonics throughout.  The cosine is a single one,
\begin{equation}
  Y_{10} = \sqrt3\,\cos\theta , \qquad \mu = \frac{Y_{10}}{\sqrt3} ,
  \label{eq:mu-as-y10}
\end{equation}
so the four products separate at once.  Multiplying $u_\text{limb}\mu$ by the pattern's constant
gives one dipole, and it is the whole of the dipole.  Multiplying it by the two quadrupoles gives
$l = 1$ and $l = 3$ terms, of order $u_\text{limb}\epsilon$; those are dropped, as is the
$u_\text{limb}$ scaling of the quadrupoles themselves, which is of the same order.  What survives
is a constant, a dipole and two quadrupoles --- no Gaunt coefficient anywhere, because the only
coupling the factor introduces is in the terms just dropped.  Written out term by term, with
$Y_{00} = 1$,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    I(\uvecr) \;=\;& \bigl[(1-u_\text{limb})\,(1-2\espin)\bigr]\,Y_{00}
      \;+\; \frac{u_\text{limb}}{\sqrt3}\,Y_{10}(\uvecr) \\
    &\;-\; \frac{4}{5}\sum_{m}\Bigl[\,\etide\,Y_{2m}^*(\uvectide)
      - \espin\,Y_{2m}^*(\uvecspin)\,\Bigr]\,Y_{2m}(\uvecr) ,
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:limb-expansion}
\end{equation}
so the coefficients are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    a_{00} &= (1-u_\text{limb})\,(1-2\espin) , \qquad
    a_{10} = \frac{u_\text{limb}}{\sqrt3} , \\
    a_{2m} &= -\frac{4}{5}\Bigl[\,\etide\,Y_{2m}^*(\uvectide)
    - \espin\,Y_{2m}^*(\uvecspin)\,\Bigr] .
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:limb-coefficients}
\end{equation}
What makes the omission defensible is that the row is a model chosen for algebra rather than a
truncation of a physical law, and that $a_{10}$ carries no deformation at all: at zero tide and
spin the row is exact, which is the zero-deformation test of
section~\ref{sec:models-worth}.

At zero deformation that row is the classical limb-darkened disk, and its answer follows from one
standard integral,
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^1 (1-\rho^{2})^{1/2} J_0(x\rho)\,\rho\,d\rho
  \;=\; \sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\,\frac{J_{3/2}(x)}{x^{3/2}}
  \;=\; \frac{j_1(x)}{x} ,
  \label{eq:limb-disk-integral}
\end{equation}
so that with $I = 1 - u_\text{limb}(1-\mu)$ and $x = 2\pi q$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{V(q)}{V(0)} \;=\;
  \frac{2\bigl[(1-u_\text{limb})\,J_1(x)/x \;+\; u_\text{limb}\,j_1(x)/x\bigr]}
       {(1-u_\text{limb}) \;+\; \tfrac23 u_\text{limb}} .
  \label{eq:limb-disk-visibility}
\end{equation}
The two terms are the $l = 0$ and $l = 1$ rows at $\epsilon = 0$: the uniform disk's $J_1$, and the
limb's $j_1$, which is the odd family's single spherical Bessel.  At $u_\text{limb} = 0$ this is
$2J_1(x)/x$, the anchor of section~\ref{sec:mode-visibility}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[$\checkmark$] \code{check_limb_darkened_disk} evaluates the row at $\epsilon = 0$ and
    compares $V(q)/V(0)$ with \eqref{eq:limb-disk-visibility} over a range of $q$, against
    scipy's Bessel functions rather than ours.  It also checks the row's value at the origin
    against $2\pi[\tfrac12(1-u_\text{limb}) + \tfrac13u_\text{limb}]$, which fixes the
    normalisation the ratio is taken with.
    \\[1pt] \vtag{28}\quad \emph{Numerical}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why the limb is not done analytically.}  The original plan was to expand the star
and the limb-darkening factor separately and multiply them with Gaunt coefficients, which
would be fully analytic.  On the disk that product is cheaper than it looks: for a disk seen
face-on
\begin{equation}
  \mu \;=\; \sqrt{1-\rho^2}
  \label{eq:limb-mu-disk}
\end{equation}
is a function of the impact parameter alone, so a limb law is a radial profile, and an
expansion in Zernike modes with $m = 0$ needs no Gaunt at all --- $\mu$ is already the
variable the basis is built from.  That is why the classical visibility of a linearly
limb-darkened disk is a two-term combination of Bessel functions.  On the sphere $\mu$ is
instead a degree-one factor, and re-expanding its product with the map's harmonics needs the
$2\times1 \to 1$ and $2\times1 \to 3$ coefficients, which is where the Gaunt coefficients would
enter.

What settled the question was not the limb but the shape.  Spin and tide make the silhouette
and the brightness both depend on $\epsilon$, with no closed form for the level surface, so
the analytic route has no route through the shape.  The row that is implemented therefore fits
the rendered image instead, and carries the limb as a factor rather than as a product of
expansions.

\subsection{The fitted row}
\label{sec:deformation-fitted}

\paragraph{The model in image space.}  By \eqref{eq:deformation-affine} the transform of the
image
\begin{equation}
  \tilde I(x,y) \;=\; \sum_{lm} a_{lm}\,\Psi_{lm}(x,y) ,
  \qquad \Psi_{lm}(x,y) \;=\; \Yup_{lm}\bigl(\Lambda^{-1}(x,y)\bigr) ,
  \label{eq:fitted-model-image}
\end{equation}
is the affine visibility, so this is the row in the form the fit uses, with $\Psi_{lm}$ the disk
basis of section~\ref{sec:visibility} carried through the stretch.

\paragraph{The fit.}  The image fitted to is the $I(x,y)$ of
section~\ref{sec:deformation-exact}, evaluated at each sample; no $\Lambda$ enters it, the stretch
being in $\Psi$ alone.  Least squares in the image plane gives the
normal equations
\begin{equation}
  G\,\mathbf{a} \;=\; \mathbf{b} ,
  \qquad
  G_{lm,l'm'} \;=\; \iint \Psi_{lm}\,\Psi_{l'm'}^*\,dx\,dy ,
  \qquad
  b_{lm} \;=\; \iint I(x,y)\,\Psi_{lm}^*\,dx\,dy ,
  \label{eq:fitted-normal-equations}
\end{equation}
both integrals over the sky plane.  $G$ carries no data: it is block diagonal in $m$, with blocks
of $3$, $4$, $4$, $2$, $2$ --- fifteen real parameters, matching $1+5+9$.  The renderer returns
samples rather than a function, so both integrals are sums over them, weighted by the sky area
each sample projects to; the basis is unchanged, and no image grid is built.

Quoted relative to the map's own norm, the residual at the optimum is
\begin{equation}
  \rho \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\sum_s w_s\bigl(I_s - \tilde I_s\bigr)^{2}}{\sum_s w_s I_s^{2}}} ,
  \label{eq:relative-residual}
\end{equation}
the truncation floor given the shape.  A $\rho$ of $10^{-2}$ therefore means the row is good to a
per cent of the map.  The same definition scores any coefficient set, which is what makes the rows
comparable, and what \eqref{eq:row-error} and its consequences use for the first-order row of
section~\ref{sec:deformation-first-order}.

\paragraph{Bookkeeping.}  The map is real, so $a_{l,-m} = a_{lm}^*$ and only
$m \ge 0$ is fitted: one design function $\Psi_{l0}$ for $m = 0$, and the pair
$\mathrm{Re}\,\Psi_{lm}$, $\mathrm{Im}\,\Psi_{lm}$ for $m > 0$.  Odd $l$ is excluded, and not for
convenience: the parity argument of section~\ref{sec:deformation-brightness} forbids it, so fitting it
would only absorb noise.

\paragraph{What the row costs, and what it assumes.}  The projection is paid once per parameter
set --- $O(N(l+1)^2)$ for $N$ samples --- and each frequency then costs a short Bessel sum over
the $(l+1)^2$ coefficients, which is the resource that is cheap.  Nothing in the per-frequency
path depends on the sampling.

Two assumptions are worth stating, because they bound what the row can be trusted for.  The
model image is $F(\Lambda^{-1}x)$ on the unit disc, so the \emph{model's} silhouette is exactly
the stretched circle, while the data's is the true deformed silhouette, which is not an ellipse;
the fit absorbs the difference into the coefficients rather than resolving it.  And $\Lambda$ comes from the three-point ellipsoid of
section~\ref{sec:deformation-axes}, so a different but equally defensible $\Lambda$ changes the
coefficients --- and because the fitted row's brightness is painted onto the surface, they are
meaningful only at the orientation they were fitted for.

The row is therefore already a disk series, $\sum_{lm}a_{lm}\Yup_{lm}$ composed with the
stretch.  A basis whose transforms were cheaper --- a Zernike series, say --- would be a different
choice of basis in the same construction, not a different construction.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[$\checkmark$] \code{check_fitted_round_trip} manufactures the samples from a known
    coefficient set with the disk's own quadrature weights, so every step is an identity and the
    fitted coefficients must return at roundoff.  It pins the unstretching, the real pairing and the
    normalisation at once, with no renderer and no sampling noise to hide behind.
    \\[1pt] \vtag{26}\quad \emph{Algebraic}
  \item[$\checkmark$] \code{check_disk_gram} pins the basis by its inner products over the disk: the
    $l = m = 0$ one is $\pi$ exactly, the disk's area, since $\Yup_{00} = 1$; entries between
    different orders vanish by the orthogonality of the angular factors; and the whole matrix agrees
    with a brute-force midpoint rule that shares nothing with the Gauss--Legendre construction but
    the definition.
    \\[1pt] \vtag{27}\quad \emph{Numerical}
  \item[$\checkmark$] The residual is evaluated by two routes --- through the normal equations and by
    expanding the quadratic directly --- and the two must agree: they differ only through the
    identity $\mathbf{a} = G^{-1}\mathbf{b}$, so a stray factor would leave the coefficients
    plausible and show up there.
    \\[1pt] \emph{Untagged}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What the numbers mean.}  The fitted $a_{lm}$ are \emph{not} the pattern's own coefficients, the closed form of
section~\ref{sec:deformation-first-order}: they are the coefficients of the unstretched image in the disk
basis.  For a sphere the two coincide, which is exactly what the round trip tests.  With $\Lambda$
fixed, the residual is the truncation floor \emph{given} the ellipsoidal shape, so comparing it with
the affine row's error separates the two errors that row conflates --- the shape model's own and the
truncation at $l \le 2$.  It is not the floor of the class: that would need $\Lambda$ fitted too, and
$\Lambda$ is only weakly identifiable, its limit being independent of it.  Finally, the five condition numbers of $G$ are what to watch as the
mode count grows; they are properties of the basis alone, fixed once rather than per parameter set.

\subsection{How good the first-order row is}
\label{sec:first-order-residual}

Write $g_\text{lin} = 1 - 2\espin - 4q$ for the row's brightness before the limb
factor, with $q$ its quadrupole part: the constant and the quadrupole separate cleanly, and the
limb factor treats them differently.  The row applies the limb factor
\eqref{eq:limb-factor} to the constant and the limb dipole and leaves the quadrupoles bare,
which is what \eqref{eq:limb-coefficients} says.  Its two errors are then
\begin{equation}
  I_\text{exact} - \tilde I \;=\;
    \bigl(1 - u_\text{limb}(1 - z/r)\bigr)\bigl(g - g_\text{lin}\bigr)
    \;-\; u_\text{limb}(1 - z/r)\,q
  \label{eq:row-error}
\end{equation}
both brightnesses: the first of order $\epsilon^2$, the linearisation of $g$; the second of order
$u_\text{limb}\epsilon$, the limb factor that the quadrupoles never receive.

The residual is the $\rho$ of \eqref{eq:relative-residual}, with the row's brightness
$I^\text{row}_s$ in place of the fitted model's: the same weights, the same norm, so the two rows
are scored by the same instrument.

The row's brightness must be matched to the sampled surface's weighted mean before the
difference is taken.  The rows are normalised at the origin (D22), so their absolute brightness
is a convention rather than a model, and the surface's mean brightness differs from an analytic
row's at first order in the deformation; without that match $\rho$ measures the flux mismatch,
which is first order and swamps the linearisation entirely.  The match is the freedom the fit
exercises when it chooses its own constant, so $\rho$ is then in the same norm as the fitted
row's --- and the fit, being optimal over a span that contains the first-order coefficients,
must have the smaller of the two.  Its two terms scale
differently in the amplitudes,
\begin{equation}
  \rho \;\simeq\; A\,\epsilon^2 \;+\; B\,u_\text{limb}\,\epsilon
  \label{eq:rho-scaling}
\end{equation}
with $A$ and $B$ independent of them.  Halving both amplitudes, and letting the field follow so
that the level surface relaxes with it, divides the first term by four and the second by two:
$\rho(\epsilon/2)/\rho(\epsilon)$ is $1/4$ at $u_\text{limb} = 0$ and tends to $1/2$ as the limb
term dominates.  At $\epsilon \approx 0.03$ and $u_\text{limb} = 0.6$ it dominates by an order of
magnitude, so the ratio is informative only with the limb off.

With the limb off the leading correction is the cubic, and the ratio is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\rho(\epsilon/2)}{\rho(\epsilon)}
  \;=\; \frac{Q/4 + C/8}{Q + C}
  \;=\; \frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1 + C/2Q}{1 + C/Q}
  \label{eq:halving-with-cubic}
\end{equation}
with $Q$ and $C$ the quadratic and cubic parts of the error, both independent of the amplitudes.
So the ratio approaches $\tfrac14$ as $\epsilon \to 0$ and lies below it whenever the cubic shares
the quadratic's sign; at the working amplitudes it reads about $0.23$, which puts the cubic at a
fifth of the quadratic.  What it tests is the order rather than the value --- $\tfrac12$ would
mean a term linear in $\epsilon$ had survived, and $1$ that the number does not scale at all.

Two quantities are \emph{not} halved with the amplitudes: $u_\text{limb}$, which describes the
atmosphere rather than the deformation, and $\Lambda$, which is not a parameter but follows from
the level radii.  Holding either fixed changes the experiment.
